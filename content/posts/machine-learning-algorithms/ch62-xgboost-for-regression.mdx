The [previous post](/machine-learning-algorithms/ch61-introduction-to-xgboost) covered XGBoost's flexibility, speed, and performance engineering at a high level without touching the one piece that's genuinely new: how its trees actually get built. This post fixes that, working a complete regression example by hand on a tiny dataset, then checking every number against a real fitted model.

## 1. Same Recipe, One New Ingredient

XGBoost solves a regression problem with the exact same skeleton as [plain gradient boosting](/machine-learning-algorithms/ch58-gradient-boosting-regression-intuition): start with a baseline (the mean of the target), fit a tree to the current residuals, scale that tree's output by a learning rate and add it to the running prediction, then repeat. If someone asks "how does XGBoost do regression," the honest one-line answer is "the same way gradient boosting does."

The one real difference is how each tree gets built. A vanilla decision tree picks splits using gini impurity, entropy, or variance reduction. XGBoost's trees use a different criterion entirely, one built around a quantity called the **similarity score**, which has a regularization term baked directly into it. That's the actual new mechanic this post works through.

## 2. The Data and the Stage 0 Baseline

A tiny placement dataset: 4 students, CGPA as the only input feature, package (in LPA) as the target.

| CGPA | Package |
|------|---------|
| 6.7  | 4.5     |
| 9.0  | 11.0    |
| 7.5  | 6.0     |
| 5.0  | 8.0     |

Same as gradient boosting, stage 0 is just the mean of the target, no feature involved:

$$
F_0 = \frac{4.5 + 11.0 + 6.0 + 8.0}{4} = 7.375
$$

Every student gets predicted 7.375 regardless of CGPA, and the pseudo-residual for each row is actual minus predicted:

| CGPA | Package | Residual |
|------|---------|----------|
| 6.7  | 4.5     | -2.875   |
| 9.0  | 11.0    | 3.625    |
| 7.5  | 6.0     | -1.375   |
| 5.0  | 8.0     | 0.625    |

Worth noticing before moving on: these four residuals sum to exactly zero. That's not a coincidence specific to this dataset, subtracting the mean from every value always produces residuals that sum to zero, it's a direct consequence of what a mean is. This fact becomes useful in the next section.

## 3. The Similarity Score

To decide where to split, XGBoost computes a similarity score for whatever group of residuals sits at a node:

$$
\text{Similarity} = \frac{\left(\sum \text{residuals}\right)^2}{n + \lambda}
$$

$n$ is the number of residuals in that group, and $\lambda$ is a regularization hyperparameter (set to $0$ throughout this post, its actual effect on overfitting is worth its own post once the math behind it is derived). Squaring the summed residuals means a group of residuals that mostly cancel out (some positive, some negative) scores low, while a group that's mostly one-directional scores high, similarity here means "how much these residuals agree in sign and size," not similarity in the everyday sense.

Applying this to all four residuals together, at the root, before any split:

$$
\text{Similarity}_{\text{root}} = \frac{(-2.875 + 3.625 - 1.375 + 0.625)^2}{4 + 0} = \frac{0^2}{4} = 0
$$

The root's similarity score is exactly zero, a direct consequence of Section 2's observation that the residuals sum to zero. This will matter in a moment: it means the "gain" calculation below simplifies for this particular tree's root.

## 4. Finding the Best Root Split

Splitting works exactly like a vanilla decision tree's threshold search: sort the feature, take the midpoint between every consecutive pair of values, and try each one as a candidate split.

$$
\text{sorted CGPA: } 5.0, \ 6.7, \ 7.5, \ 9.0 \implies \text{candidates: } 5.85, \ 7.1, \ 8.25
$$

For each candidate, compute the similarity score of the left group (SSL) and right group (SSR), then the **gain** from splitting:

$$
\text{Gain} = \text{SSL} + \text{SSR} - \text{Similarity}_{\text{parent}}
$$

| Split (CGPA below) | Left residuals | Right residuals | SSL | SSR | Gain |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5.85 | `{0.625}` | `{-2.875, 3.625, -1.375}` | 0.391 | 0.130 | 0.521 |
| 7.1  | `{-2.875, 0.625}` | `{3.625, -1.375}` | 2.531 | 2.531 | 5.063 |
| 8.25 | `{-2.875, -1.375, 0.625}` | `{3.625}` | 4.380 | 13.141 | **17.521** |

Since $\text{Similarity}_{\text{parent}} = 0$ (Section 3), gain here is just SSL + SSR, nothing to subtract. The split at **CGPA below 8.25** wins by a wide margin, mostly because it isolates the single largest residual (3.625, the CGPA = 9.0 row) into its own leaf, and an unshared residual squared over $n=1$ produces a large similarity score.

## 5. Growing a Second Level

The right side of that split (CGPA $\geq$ 8.25, just the one row with residual 3.625) has nothing left to split. The left side has three rows and two remaining candidate thresholds, 5.85 and 7.1:

| Split (CGPA below) | Left residuals | Right residuals | SSL | SSR | Gain |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5.85 | `{0.625}` | `{-2.875, -1.375}` | 0.391 | 9.031 | **5.042** |
| 7.1  | `{-2.875, 0.625}` | `{-1.375}` | 2.531 | 1.891 | 0.042 |

5.85 wins clearly, again because it isolates a lone residual (0.625, the CGPA = 5.0 row) into its own single-row leaf. The finished tree, capped at depth 2 (XGBoost's own default is depth 6, but a 4-row toy dataset has no business growing that deep):

```
CGPA < 8.25?
├── No  → leaf: {9.0}                    (residual 3.625)
└── Yes → CGPA < 5.85?
          ├── Yes → leaf: {5.0}           (residual 0.625)
          └── No  → leaf: {6.7, 7.5}      (residuals -2.875, -1.375)
```

## 6. Leaf Output Values

Once the structure is fixed, each leaf needs an actual number to output. The formula is close to the similarity score's numerator, but without the square:

$$
\text{Output} = \frac{\sum \text{residuals}}{n + \lambda}
$$

With $\lambda = 0$, this is just the average of the residuals in that leaf, same as a vanilla regression tree:

| Leaf | Residuals | Output |
|------|-----------|--------|
| CGPA $\geq$ 8.25 | `{3.625}` | 3.625 |
| $5.85 \leq \text{CGPA} < 8.25$ | `{-2.875, -1.375}` | -2.125 |
| CGPA $< 5.85$ | `{0.625}` | 0.625 |

## 7. Assembling the Stage 1 Combined Model

The running prediction updates the same way it does in plain gradient boosting, baseline plus learning rate times the tree's output for that row. XGBoost's default learning rate ($\eta$) is $0.3$:

$$
F_1(x) = F_0 + \eta \cdot \text{tree}_1(x)
$$

| CGPA | $F_0$ | Leaf output | $F_1$ | Package | New residual |
|------|-------|-------------|-------|---------|--------------|
| 6.7  | 7.375 | -2.125 | $7.375 + 0.3(-2.125) = 6.7375$ | 4.5  | -2.2375 |
| 9.0  | 7.375 | 3.625  | $7.375 + 0.3(3.625) = 8.4625$  | 11.0 | 2.5375  |
| 7.5  | 7.375 | -2.125 | $7.375 + 0.3(-2.125) = 6.7375$ | 6.0  | -0.7375 |
| 5.0  | 7.375 | 0.625  | $7.375 + 0.3(0.625) = 7.5625$  | 8.0  | 0.4375  |

Compare the new residuals against Section 2's originals, every single one shrank toward zero (-2.875 → -2.2375, 3.625 → 2.5375, -1.375 → -0.7375, 0.625 → 0.4375). That's the whole point of a boosting stage: nudge every prediction a bit closer to correct, then repeat with a second tree fit on these smaller residuals, and a third, and so on.

## 8. Checking This Against a Real XGBRegressor

Hand arithmetic across three sections is exactly the kind of thing worth double-checking against real code rather than trusting by eye. Replicating the exact-greedy split search above in plain Python and comparing it to a real `XGBRegressor` fit with matching hyperparameters:

```python
from xgboost import XGBRegressor
import numpy as np

cgpa = np.array([6.7, 9.0, 7.5, 5.0]).reshape(-1, 1)
package = np.array([4.5, 11.0, 6.0, 8.0])

model = XGBRegressor(
    n_estimators=1, max_depth=2, learning_rate=0.3,
    reg_lambda=0, base_score=package.mean(),
    min_child_weight=0, gamma=0, tree_method="exact",
)
model.fit(cgpa, package)
print(model.predict(cgpa))
```

```
[6.7375 8.4625 6.7375 7.5625]
```

That matches Section 7's hand-derived $F_1$ predictions exactly, confirming the similarity-score/gain/leaf-output formulas above are the actual mechanism, not just an approximation of one.

## 9. A Note on How Splits Get Searched

The threshold search in Section 4 and Section 5, sort the feature, try the midpoint of every consecutive pair, is called the **exact greedy algorithm**, and it's what XGBoost's original paper describes as the default approach: accurate, since it genuinely checks every possible split, but expensive on a feature with millions of distinct values, since every one of those becomes a candidate. For large datasets, XGBoost falls back to an **approximate algorithm** instead, which buckets candidate splits rather than trying every single value, similar in spirit to the quantile-based binning covered in the flexibility/speed overview. That approximate method deserves its own dedicated treatment rather than a mention here.

## 10. What to Take Away

- XGBoost's regression procedure is structurally identical to plain gradient boosting: baseline (mean), fit a tree to the residuals, scale by a learning rate, update, repeat.
- The actual difference is how each tree is built: splits are chosen by maximizing **gain** (SSL + SSR - SS_parent), where similarity score is $(\sum \text{residuals})^2 / (n + \lambda)$, not by gini, entropy, or variance reduction.
- A leaf's output value is $\sum \text{residuals} / (n + \lambda)$, the same as a plain average when $\lambda = 0$.
- Residuals from a mean baseline always sum to zero, which is why a stage-1 tree's root similarity score is always zero, and gain reduces to just SSL + SSR for that first tree.
- The default learning rate is $0.3$ and the default max tree depth is $6$, though a small toy dataset has no reason to use either default at full strength.
- This exact-greedy split search (try every possible threshold) is one of two split-finding strategies XGBoost supports, the other, an approximate quantile-based method for large datasets, is a topic on its own.
