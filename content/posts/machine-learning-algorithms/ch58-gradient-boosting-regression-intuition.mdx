Gradient boosting is a boosting algorithm, same family as [AdaBoost](/machine-learning-algorithms/ch54-adaboost-geometric-intuition): small models added one at a time, each one aware of what the ones before it got wrong. What changes is *how* that awareness gets passed forward. AdaBoost reweights rows so the next stump pays more attention to the ones it missed. Gradient boosting takes a more direct route: it hands the next model the previous model's error itself, as a number to predict. This post covers the regression case and stays at the intuition level, exactly how the source video sequences it; the math behind *why* this works is deferred to a follow-up post, same as the plan the video itself lays out.

## 1. Stage 1 Isn't a Model, It's a Number

Ask what the very first "model" in a gradient boosting regressor looks like, and the answer is anticlimactic: it's the **mean of the target column**. Not a tree, not a linear fit, just a single number that gets returned for every row regardless of its features.

Take a toy dataset of 5 students with a known salary (in LPA): 3, 4, 8, 6, 3. Stage 1's prediction for *every* student, no matter their IQ or CGPA, is:

$$
\text{model}_1 = \frac{3+4+8+6+3}{5} = 4.8
$$

That's it. Why the mean specifically, rather than the median or something else, is the subject of the next post's derivation; for now it's enough to know that stage 1 is a constant, and every subsequent stage's whole job is to chip away at how wrong that constant is for each individual row.

## 2. The Pseudo-Residual: What Gets Passed Forward

Once stage 1 exists, its error per row is just actual minus predicted:

| Student | Actual salary | Model 1 prediction | Residual |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3 | 4.8 | -1.8 |
| 2 | 4 | 4.8 | -0.8 |
| 3 | 8 | 4.8 | 3.2 |
| 4 | 6 | 4.8 | 1.2 |
| 5 | 3 | 4.8 | -1.8 |

This is called a **pseudo-residual**, "pseudo" because it borrows the name from ordinary linear regression's residual ($y - \hat{y}$) without being derived the same way, it's a stand-in for a more general quantity (the negative gradient of the loss function) that the math post will make precise. For now, actual minus predicted is the working definition.

## 3. Stage 2 Predicts the Error, Not the Salary

Here's the part that's easy to get backwards: **stage 2 is a decision tree trained on the same input features (IQ, CGPA), but its target column is the residual, not the salary.** You're not asking "what's this student's salary", you're asking "how wrong was stage 1 for this student, based on their IQ and CGPA". If stage 2 gets good at predicting *that*, the combined model (stage 1 + stage 2) automatically gets better at predicting the actual salary, without stage 2 ever being told what a salary is.

With this toy dataset small enough for a tree to fit exactly, stage 2 reproduces each row's residual perfectly. That means stage 1 + stage 2 reproduces the training salaries exactly too: 4.8 + (-1.8) = 3, 4.8 + (-0.8) = 4, matching the actual values row for row. That looks like a win, but it's actually a warning sign: a model that reproduces its training data exactly is **overfitting**, it will do the same thing on a brand-new student it hasn't seen and get it badly wrong, since real data won't hand it the exact residual to add back.

## 4. The Fix: a Shared Learning Rate

Gradient boosting's answer to that overfitting is a **learning rate**, typically 0.1, that scales down every stage after the first:

$$
\hat{y} = \text{model}_1 + \eta \cdot \text{model}_2 + \eta \cdot \text{model}_3 + \dots
$$

With $\eta = 0.1$ instead of the implicit 1 from the previous section, student 1's prediction becomes $4.8 + 0.1 \times (-1.8) = 4.62$ instead of exactly 3. That's a much smaller, more cautious step toward the actual value. The next stage's residual is now $3 - 4.62 = -1.62$, still nonzero (as opposed to the 0 you'd get from a full step), but visibly closer to 0 than the original -1.8. Recomputing this over the whole toy set: residual 1 → residual 2 goes -1.8 → -1.62, -0.8 → -0.72, 3.2 → 2.88, each row's error shrinking a little, not vanishing all at once.

That's the whole training loop: fit a tree on the current residual, take a small step in that tree's direction (scaled by $\eta$), recompute the residual, repeat. More stages push every residual closer to 0 gradually rather than any single stage jumping straight to a perfect (and overfit) fit. **The learning rate is the same number for every stage**, unlike AdaBoost's α, which is computed fresh per stage based on how good that specific stump was. Gradient boosting's stages all get the same discount; what changes stage to stage is what each stage is trying to correct, not how much it's trusted.

## 6. Why the Learning Rate Actually Matters

Section 4 argued for a smaller learning rate in the abstract; here's what it costs concretely. Running the same 100-row dataset for the same 5 added trees, once with `learning_rate = 1` and once with `learning_rate = 0.1`:

<img src="/images/ch58-learning-rate-comparison.png" alt="Two scatter plots side by side of the same quadratic dataset with 5 trees added. Left, learning rate 1, the red fitted curve hugs the U-shape tightly, training residual sum of squares 0.079, including the same overfitting spike near X=0.1. Right, learning rate 0.1, the red fitted curve is much flatter and further from the U-shape, training residual sum of squares 2.122, clearly underfit after only 5 stages." className="w-full h-auto" />

At `learning_rate = 1`, 5 trees are already enough to hug the training data tightly, overfitting spike included. At `learning_rate = 0.1`, those same 5 trees have only taken small steps and are still visibly underfit, training error over 25 times higher. Neither is "correct" in isolation, a small learning rate needs many more stages to reach the same fit a large one reaches quickly, which is exactly the trade-off the video points at: smaller steps in the right direction, more of them, rather than one big step that overshoots into memorization.

## 7. Gradient Boosting vs. AdaBoost: Two Concrete Differences

With both algorithms now covered, the contrast is specific, not just "both do boosting":

- **Tree size.** AdaBoost uses decision stumps, `max_depth = 1`. Gradient boosting uses noticeably bigger trees, typically `max_leaf_nodes` between 8 and 32 (8 was used above, since the dataset is small).
- **Per-stage weighting.** AdaBoost computes a distinct α per stage from that stage's error rate, some stumps count more than others in the final vote. Gradient boosting uses one shared learning rate across every stage; no stage is individually up- or down-weighted relative to the others, the only thing that changes between stages is what target (which residual) each one is trained on.

## 8. What to Take Away

- Stage 1 of a gradient boosting regressor is just the mean of the target column, not a trained model.
- Every later stage is a tree trained to predict the *current residual* (actual minus the ensemble's prediction so far), not the original target.
- Final prediction is $\text{model}_1 + \eta \sum \text{model}_i$ for $i \geq 2$, with the same $\eta$ applied to every stage.
- Without a learning rate below 1, a tree can fit the training residuals so well that the combined model reproduces the training targets exactly, a clear overfitting signal, not a good outcome.
- A smaller learning rate needs more stages to reach the same fit; it trades training speed for a path that's less likely to memorize noise along the way.
